# Stack size changes the plan

The value of your cards matters, but so does the number of big blinds in your stack.
{: .ff-page-intro }

## Tonight’s goal

Players should estimate their stack in big blinds and recognise whether they are comfortable, medium or short.

## The ten-minute lesson

### 1 minute — ask

Ask: “Is a stack of 1,000 chips large?”

At blinds of 25/50 it is 20 big blinds. At 200/400 it is only 2.5. Chip count alone does not tell the story.

### 3 minutes — count big blinds

Use:

> **Stack ÷ big blind = number of big blinds**

At 100/200:

| Stack | Big blinds | Simple label |
|--:|--:|:--|
| 6,000 | 30 BB | Comfortable |
| 3,000 | 15 BB | Medium |
| 1,600 | 8 BB | Short |

### 3 minutes — change the plan

- **Comfortable:** room to raise, fold and play later streets.
- **Medium:** choose hands carefully; losing one large pot matters.
- **Short:** avoid paying chips to “have a look.” Look for a strong opportunity to commit decisively.

Also notice opponents’ stacks. A player can only win from you what they have in front of them.

### 2 minutes — demonstrate

Give A♠ J♦ to three players with 30 BB, 15 BB and 6 BB. Discuss why the same hand may lead to different choices depending on position and earlier action.

Do not teach a rigid all-in chart yet. Teach awareness.

### 1 minute — set the challenge

> **Tonight’s mission:** At the start of every blind level, estimate your stack in big blinds.

Award the **Stack Captain** recognition for consistently knowing their situation.

## During the game

Ask:

- “How many big blinds do you have?”
- “Can you make this call and still have a playable stack?”
- “Which opponent can put you all-in?”
- “Did the new blind level change your plan?”

{: .table_tip }
Being short-stacked is a situation, not a failure. Clear decisions become more important as the stack gets smaller.

## Hand of the night

Compare how two different stack sizes would play the same starting hand. Which options disappear as the stack becomes shorter?
